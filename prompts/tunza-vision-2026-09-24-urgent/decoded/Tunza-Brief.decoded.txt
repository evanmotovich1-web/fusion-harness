Tunza  the brief
"Healthcare anywhere, anytime." The decision before the clinic: when someone is sick and care is far away, the question is not what is
the diagnosis? but what should happen next?
1. What Tunza is (one paragraph)
Tunza is a bilingual AI symptom-triage product for rural, low-income communities  first built for Kenya. A person (or a Community Health
Promoter, CHP) enters symptoms, optional vitals and a photo; Tunza returns possible conditions, an urgency level ( self-care / see-clinic /
urgent), plain-language next steps and red-flag warning signs, then finds the nearest facility. Doctors have accounts, a realtime dashboard
of urgent cases, and push notifications. The long-term product is a case-prioritisation engine for Kenya's eCHIS so overwhelmed clinics
see the sickest child first.

6. Classification vs ranking  and why Tunza is a ranker
A classifier answers "urgent or not?" for one case with a threshold. A ranker orders all open cases by risk; the facility takes the top K it can
absorb today. Ranking metrics: precision@K (of the K you sent, how many were truly urgent), recall@K (of all truly urgent, how many were
in the top K), AUC (probability a random urgent case scores above a random non-urgent one). Because K varies by facility, you need a
calibrated score and you report precision@K at each site's K.

At low prevalence this is the lever on precision, not sensitivity.

Of cases flagged urgent, % that truly were.
The number the CHP and the clinic feel. Too low = alert fatigue = app ignored.
Prevalence (base rate)
% of all cases that are truly urgent.
Set by the population, not by you. It dominates precision.
8. The base-rate trap (placeholder numbers  substitute your own; the shape survives)
Take 10,000 child visits at 2% prevalence = 200 truly urgent. A model with 90% sensitivity / 90% specificity flags 180 of the 200 true urgents
 and also flags 10% of the 9,800 non-urgent = 980 false urgents. Precision = 180 / 1,160 » 15%: about six false alarms for every real one.
Raise specificity to 99% and false urgents fall to 98 ® precision » 65%. Raise sensitivity to 99% instead and precision barely moves (»17%).
Lesson: when the thing you hunt is rare, specificity  not sensitivity  controls whether humans keep trusting the tool.
Reference failure to cite: the Epic sepsis model  external validation found AUC » 0.63, PPV » 12%, fired on ~18% of admissions; clinicians stopped
looking. Same structure Tunza faces at scale.
9. How an LLM prompt differs from a trained model
Today (prompt): zero data needed, ships instantly, general medical knowledge, conservative by instruction. Cannot learn your
population, cannot be calibrated, no asset you own, per-call cost, and you cannot prove a number for it without an eval set.
Tomorrow (trained ranker): needs labelled outcomes, learns local prevalence and presentation, produces calibrated scores, is an
owned asset  and inherits every bias in the data you collected.
Middle path (the honest pitch): rules (IMCI/ETAT) for hard danger signs + LLM for language and next-steps + a logged outcome
record. The record is the year-two model's training set.

11. Risks already named to you (accept or reject  not settled)
Feedback-loop bias. If outcomes are only observed for cases the model ranked high, the model confirms itself forever; every eval looks
great while low-ranked children are missed. Same as a loan-default model trained only on approved applicants. Defence: observe
outcomes on a random sample regardless of rank.
Survivorship bias in self-report. Families least likely to type an outcome are the ones whose child got much worse. Passive self-report
drifts the data toward "everything went fine." Defence: active follow-up on a random sample  a real cost line missing from every deck.
Base rates beat model quality (section 8). Plan the precision floor before the first pilot, or the CHP stops opening the app.
Distribution shift. A model trained in one county, season, or clinic mix degrades elsewhere. Split by facility/time; re-check calibration per
site.
Data rights. eCHIS data is Kenyan MoH property. "Connected to eCHIS" is not "licensed to train on eCHIS." Kenya's Data Protection Act
2019 applies. The claimed asset depends on a data-sharing agreement nobody has named yet.

13. How to say it in a room (30-second version)
"Kenya has put 100k+ community health promoters into ~9M households and digitised their reporting through eCHIS  but eCHIS cannot
tell a clinic which child to see first. Tunza ships day one on published WHO danger-sign protocol plus a constrained language model, in the
user's language, and logs every case and outcome. That outcome record  gathered through an NGO partner under a data-sharing
