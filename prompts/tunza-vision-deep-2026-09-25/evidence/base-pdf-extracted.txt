Tunza is being designed to help people get the care they need: understand what is happening,
choose the next step, and make sure it happens. A family needs clear advice. A community health
promoter, a health worker serving local households, needs to know who needs help. The clinic
needs enough information to respond.
Kenya is connecting health services and improving how they share records. But information alone
does not get someone to a clinic, confirm a place for them, or bring a test result back. Tunza would
help connect those steps.
WHO's 2025 Kenya report cites an estimated 355 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births and a
92% shortage of doctors. These figures describe Kenya's health challenges, not Tunza's results.
When care is stretched, getting people to the right service without delay matters even more.
The Ministry of Health reported 10,277 facilities connected to national systems in January 2026 and
more than 107,000 community health promoters deployed in May. Tunza would need to work with
this network; we do not yet have the access or agreements to claim that connection.
The Ministry's March 2026 work on referral policy calls for better communication and clearer
responsibility between services. It also covers moving test samples and sharing specialist
expertise. Working with local health workers early would help Tunza fit the way care is actually
delivered.
Tunza could also help arrange for a blood sample to reach the right lab, get results back to a doctor,
or find a clinic with the vaccine someone needs. The aim is to connect people, samples, results,
and services. Availability would need confirmation from the provider.
A nearby address is not enough. Can the clinic provide the service? Can the person get there? Has
the clinic agreed to receive them? A request, an acceptance, an arrival, and completed care are
separate steps. Each needs confirmation, with someone responsible for following up.
Today, the public demo offers English and Kiswahili screens, guided questions, and a sample
referral journey saved on the user's device. Its assessment rules have not been proven safe for real
care. Shared records, real clinic responses, and follow-up are still things we intend to build and test.
Families would see warning signs and clear instructions. Health workers would see who needs
attention and follow-up. Clinics would receive a useful summary and could accept a request,
suggest another service, or ask a question. Each person would see only the information they need.

JEV / TypeSafe is our planned set of rules for how Tunza uses AI.
The AI would ask useful questions and flag missing or unclear information.
Safety rules approved by doctors would always take priority.
If the AI cannot answer or the connection fails, Tunza would show how to get help.
Each decision would be recorded so we can find mistakes and improve.
Someone needing emergency care must not wait for an app notification. Tunza would flag
unanswered requests and show the health worker how to get help. Questions should never delay
urgent care, and a guess should never look like a confirmed fact.
We would start with one small problem chosen with local doctors and health workers. They would
help define the safety rules, the information needed, and which mistakes are unacceptable. Before
a trial, we would agree on what success looks like and when to stop.
At first, Tunza would run alongside normal care without changing it. Doctors would compare its
suggestions with actual decisions and review disagreements. Only after passing agreed checks
would we consider a small, supervised trial. Someone would be responsible for watching for
problems and acting on them.
Tests would check missed urgent cases, unnecessary referrals, warning signs, and whether the AI
admits uncertainty. English and Kiswahili would be tested separately. We would keep some cases
out of training so the final test checks more than what the AI has already been taught.
We would also check whether the next step happened: did the person receive care, did the lab get
the sample, and did the doctor receive the result? Follow-up would include people who never
completed their referral, not just those who return to the app. If we cannot find out what happened,
the result stays unknown.
A wrong assessment, failed transport, and outdated clinic information need different fixes. We
would use reviewed cases to identify the problem before changing the AI. Patient records would be
used for training only with permission and review.
We would test existing AI models before training them further. Any training would need a clear
purpose, consistent checks, a saved record, and a spending limit. Progress would mean better
decisions and completed next steps, not simply more computing power.
People will not use an app that makes care harder. Tunza is being designed to make it as easy as
possible: fewer buttons, less typing, and clear next steps. We want people to enjoy using it and get
what they need without extra effort.
We will win by moving faster, keeping things simple, and learning from every failure to make Tunza
better.
