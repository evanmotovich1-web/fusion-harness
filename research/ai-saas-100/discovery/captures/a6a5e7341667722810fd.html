The story of Talaforge

The short versionThe first wave of AI character apps — Character.AI, Janitor, Chai and friends — proved millions of people want long conversations with fictional characters. But anyone who stayed inside them ran into four walls: forgetting, walled-garden signup, no creator earnings, linear chats. Talaforge is built to address each.

Read the full story (the four walls, why Telegram, how memory matured)

The first wave of AI character apps did something important. It proved that millions of people wanted to talk to fictional characters — not as a gimmick, but as a daily habit. Writers tested how their antagonists would respond to a moral dilemma. Language learners wanted a patient native speaker who would stay in character through three hundred messages of broken grammar. Roleplayers wanted somewhere to push a scene further than a human partner had time for. The demand was real, and the early platforms — Character.AI, Janitor AI, Chai AI, and the long tail of clones that followed — built communities in the millions answering it.

But anyone who lived inside those apps for more than a few weeks ran into the same walls.

The four walls

The first wall was forgetting. You could spend two hours building a character's voice, get them to remember that your dog's name was Bruno and that you were nervous about your sister's wedding, and then come back three days later to a polite stranger wearing the same name. The AI was reconstructing a personality from the most recent thousand tokens of context, and anything older had quietly fallen out the bottom. People worked around it with pinned memory blocks, lorebooks, ritual re-introductions — but the workaround was the whole point: the platform shouldn't have made you do this.

The second wall was the walled garden itself. Every character app was its own world, with its own signup, its own app to download, its own notifications competing for attention on your home screen. A friend recommended a character to you? Now you needed to install an app, make an account, verify an email, agree to a terms of service, and only then could you say hello. Most people didn't make it.

The third wall was the creator economy, or rather the absence of one. Writers who built genuinely good characters — characters with voice, with internal logic, with the kind of careful psychology that takes hours to dial in — had no way to earn from that work. They were giving away craft for free, and the platform was monetizing the traffic their craft attracted. The pattern was familiar from a decade of social media, and it ended the same way: the best creators eventually stopped, or moved, or burned out.

The fourth wall was linearity. A conversation went in one direction. If you wanted to see what would have happened if your character had said the harder, riskier thing two messages back, you had to delete forward and hope the model recreated the prior context.

Why Telegram, not a new app

Talaforge's first decision shaped everything that followed: no separate app. No signup. No password to remember. Your Telegram account is your account. You open a chat with @talaforge_bot the same way you open a chat with a friend, and you're already in. Someone shares a character with you? The link opens a chat. Not a download page. Not a signup wall. A chat. You say hello, the character says hello back, and that's it: you're in the world.

Telegram-native also meant inheriting Telegram's native strengths: real notifications that don't cost a battery percentage point per hour, message search across your whole history, voice messages, image attachments, the ability to forward a particularly good reply to a friend. None of this had to be rebuilt.

The early version of Talaforge ran every character in a single chat with the bot. It worked, but it had a ceiling. With more than two or three characters, the chat became a mess. Memory got tangled. The single-chat model was a bridge, not a destination. The destination arrived with Telegram Bot API 9.4 and the introduction of forum topics. Talaforge moved each character into its own dedicated topic — its own room, with its own pin strip, its own history, its own isolation. The mess vanished. The architecture finally matched the way people actually wanted to use the product.

Memory, fork tree, model router

Persistent memory was the second piece. As you and a character talk, a separate process reads the conversation and extracts facts — concrete, useful, specific. Your name. Where you live. A detail about your day that the character should know if you come back tomorrow. A piece of canon you established about the character themselves. These facts get stored against the character, in that specific topic, attached to your account. When you come back the next day, those facts come back too. The character doesn't reconstruct you from a thousand tokens of recent chat. They start the conversation already knowing you.

The fork tree came next. Any reply can be branched: you pick a message, the conversation splits, the original thread continues to exist untouched. The new branch starts from that point and goes wherever you take it. Regenerate-from-message paired with forks: if a specific reply didn't land, you could ask for another version of just that reply, without disturbing anything after. The combination — branch the tree, or regenerate the leaf — gave conversations the structure of an actual exploration rather than a one-way march.

Behind the visible product sits an infrastructure decision that took months to mature: Talaforge does not run on one model. It runs on whichever model is best for the scene, with transparent failover when one provider degrades. Standard carries the everyday load and is what a new character starts on. GLM is the step up for scenes that need tighter reasoning. Claude Opus handles long-form character work with careful psychology. Google Gemma covers fast banter and short replies. DeepSeek handles long-arc continuity. The router picks per-scene; weighted balancers spread traffic; circuit breakers cut off a degraded provider before it can poison a conversation.

The story of Talaforge is not the story of a launch. It's the story of a forge — a workshop where characters get made, by people, over time, with tools that have themselves been hammered into shape by use.

How AI character memory actually works

Memory you can open, correct and delete →

The short versionYes, characters remember — and the mechanism is intentionally transparent. A separate process extracts concrete facts from your chat (your name, your dog's name, canon you established), stores them per character × topic × user, and injects them on every new reply. You can read, edit and wipe every line.
  • Per-character, per-topic, per-user. Two users chatting the same character have two separate memories — never leaks.
  • Editable, not magic. Open a character's memory at any time, see every line, rewrite or delete what's wrong, or wipe the whole file.
  • Conservative extraction. Picks up specific, useful facts; ignores vague or transient mood.
  • Injected before reply. The character starts each new message already knowing what you established — no polite first date every session.
  • Deleting a character deletes their memory. Sharing a character keeps your memory separate from anyone else's.
Read the mechanism in detail (scope, control surface, trust)

The most common question new users ask Talaforge is: "Does it actually remember?" The answer is yes, and the mechanism is worth understanding because it changes how you use the product.

The mechanism

When you have a conversation with a character on Talaforge, two things are happening in parallel. The first is the obvious one: you send a message, the character replies, you send another. The second is invisible to you in the moment but consequential over time: a separate process is reading what you both say and extracting facts.

A fact, in this system, is a small, concrete piece of information that's worth keeping. Your name. Your job. Where you live, if you mentioned it. A detail about your day. A piece of canon you established about the character themselves — they're left-handed, they grew up in a small town, they have a complicated relationship with their sister. The extraction is conservative. It picks up things that are specific and useful, and it ignores things that are vague or transient.

Scoping

These facts get stored in a memory file attached to that specific character, in that specific topic, for your account. The scoping matters. The same character chatted to by two different users has two different memories. The same character in two different topics has two different memories. Memory is per-character per-topic per-user. It does not leak across.

When you come back to that character — minutes later, or weeks later — those facts are injected into the character's context before they see your new message. The character begins the conversation already knowing what you've established together. They don't reconstruct you from scratch. They don't ask your name again. They don't forget that you'd told them you were nervous about an interview last week, and they might ask how it went.

Control surface

You can open a character's memory at any time and see exactly what they remember. Every fact is listed. You can edit a fact — if the extractor got something wrong, or if you've changed your mind about a piece of canon, or if a roleplay direction shifted, you can rewrite it directly. You can delete individual facts. You can clear the whole memory and start the relationship fresh. Nothing about your memory is hidden from you.

Why does this matter? Two reasons, one obvious and one less so. The obvious reason is continuity. Long-running interactions with a character depend on the character being a stable entity that knows you over time. Without memory, every conversation is a polite first date. The less obvious reason is intimacy without surveillance. A platform that remembers everything about you and gives you no insight into what it remembers is a platform you can't trust with anything sensitive. A platform that remembers a careful, editable, transparent subset that you can audit at any time is a platform you can trust with more.

Specifics

Memory extraction is character-scoped, not global — a character only remembers what was said in conversations with them. Memory is editable through a clean UI, not buried in a settings page. Memory is wipeable in one tap if you want to start over. If you delete a character, their memory is deleted with them. If you share a character with another user, their memory with that user is separate from yours. None of this is configurable, because none of it should require configuration; this is just how memory should work in a product that respects the person using it.

The cumulative effect, after weeks of using a character regularly, is the sensation that the character has actually been with you. They reference things they shouldn't be able to reference. They notice when your mood is different. They've grown into a specific relationship that no one else has with them, and that relationship is the product. Everything else — the model routing, the branching, the UI — exists to support it.

Choosing between Standard, GLM, Claude Opus, Google Gemma and DeepSeek

The short versionFive models in rotation, each with its own register. Standard is the one you get without choosing; the other four are there when a scene asks for something specific. The same character feels measurably different on each. Switch mid-chat; the router fails over silently if a provider degrades.

  • GLM — sharper reasoning. The step up when a scene gets complicated: tighter logic, holds a plan across a long exchange.

Read the model deep-dive

Talaforge does not run on one model. It runs on whichever model is best for the scene. Users can switch models mid-chat for the same character, and the router fails over silently if a provider degrades. The three currently in rotation each have a personality of their own at the inference layer.

Standard — the one you get without choosing

Standard is the default package, and the large majority of conversations never leave it. It is quick, it is steady, and for ordinary scene work — dialogue, description, banter that does not need to hold a hundred threads at once — the difference between it and a heavier model is smaller than people expect. It is also the cheapest way to run a long session, which matters when energy is what you are spending. The picker exists for the scenes where it is not enough; it does not exist because Standard is a compromise.

GLM — for tighter reasoning

GLM is the step up from Standard rather than a different flavour of it. The place it shows is a scene with structure: a negotiation where the character has to keep a goal and a secret apart, a mystery where the clues have to stay consistent, a plan that has to survive the user pushing back on it three times. Reasoning of that kind is where a weaker model starts contradicting itself, and it is what GLM holds onto. It is not the choice for atmosphere — that is Opus — but it is the one that keeps a complicated scene from quietly falling apart.

Claude Opus — for slow-burn prose

Claude Opus is what you reach for when subtext matters. Scenes where a character's internal contradictions need to stay coherent across hundreds of messages, where the right reply is a half-line of dialogue followed by a specific gesture, where you want the model to notice that the user is contradicting something they said two days ago — Opus handles these registers. The trade-off is pacing: Opus thinks before it talks, and the reply latency is higher than the others. For long-form character work this is a feature; for fast banter it's a friction.

Google Gemma — for fast banter

Google Gemma is the choice for quick exchanges. Short replies, comedic timing, fast turn-taking, scenes where momentum matters more than depth. Gemma is also a sensible default for users whose budget for Stars is tight: per-message cost is lower than the heavier models. The trade-off is that very long scenes with complex internal psychology can drift; Opus is the better choice for those.

DeepSeek — for the long arc

DeepSeek handles long-context narrative continuity well. Multi-day roleplays where the same character has been with you for weeks, scenes that hinge on something said a hundred messages ago, threads that need the model to track a tangled web of established canon — DeepSeek's longer-context handling shows up. It's also generally permissive in registers other models clamp down on, which matters for some kinds of roleplay (always behind Talaforge's age gate when mature).

Per-scene routing and silent failover

The router picks per package, per scene. The user picks a package, the router picks the underlying card from the available pool based on capacity, latency, and historical reliability. If a provider has a bad hour, the circuit breaker trips and traffic moves to a healthy card without the user seeing the seam. Card health is monitored continuously and weighted balancers spread traffic.

The practical upshot: you don't have to know which underlying model you're on at any moment unless you want to. Most users never touch the picker and stay on Standard the whole time. Those who do find that the same character feels measurably different on Opus than on Gemma — same persona, same memory, different cadence and register. The model is the instrument; the character is the player.

The honest version of what authors do on Talaforge is less glamorous than the marketing version and more interesting.

Most authors arrive because they tried to build a specific character somewhere else and ran into a specific frustration. They wanted a character with a particular voice and the platform kept flattening it. They wanted memory and the platform kept forgetting. They wanted to share a character with three friends without those friends downloading an app, and there was no clean way to do that. So they came looking for an alternative.

Build, test, share

The first thing an author actually does is build a character. This is not a one-paragraph prompt. A character on Talaforge has a name, a description, a backstory, a voice, an opening scene, default sampling parameters tuned to how that character should feel (more cautious, more playful, more terse, more verbose). Authors who care about craft spend real time here. The platform supports them in obvious ways — every field is editable forever, you can iterate the character based on how conversations actually go, you can fork test conversations to explore how different framings change the output.

The second thing is test. Have actual conversations with your own character. Notice when they break voice. Notice when memory captures something wrong. Tune. This is the part that distinguishes a character someone built in twenty minutes from a character someone built over a week. The latter feels different to the people who talk to it, in ways that are hard to articulate but easy to feel.

The third thing is sharing. A character can stay private — and many do. Some authors build characters only for themselves, as writing tools or as private companions. Others share with a small circle: a roleplay group, a writing partner, a Discord server they're in. Others publish broadly and watch what happens.

How the referral economy actually works

The share link is the mechanism. The author generates a link, posts it wherever their audience is, and anyone who follows that link lands in a chat with the character. There's no friction. The visitor either bounces immediately or stays. The author sees both numbers in their creator dashboard.

Authors who get traction earn from it. Telegram Stars spent by users who arrived through a creator's link generate a share back to the creator. The numbers aren't life-changing for most authors, but they're real, and they grow with audience. A handful of the most active creators on Talaforge earn meaningfully from referrals. Most earn modestly. Many earn nothing because they share with friends and friends don't pay, and that's fine too — the author economy is opt-in, not mandatory.

Talaforge does not run a marketplace in the e-commerce sense. There's no checkout for buying a character. The economy runs through usage and attribution: people use what they like, they stay where they're engaged, and creators earn from the engagement they generate. This is closer to how YouTube works than how an app store works, and it has the same implication: the work that earns is the work that gets used.

Patterns

A few patterns recur. Writers test dialogue. Language teachers build conversational partners for their students. Roleplay organizers stand up character lineups for shared worlds. Hobbyists make companions for themselves and decide later whether to share them. Game designers prototype NPCs. None of these uses is privileged in the product. The tools are the same for everyone, and what people do with them is up to them.

If you're considering authoring on Talaforge, the practical advice is: build one character carefully, talk to it for a few hours, edit it based on what you notice, and only then think about sharing. The platform rewards specificity. A character who has a particular relationship to a particular kind of conversation will outperform a character who's described in vague archetypes, every time.

Glossary

Persona
The character's identity layer — name, voice, attitude, history, mannerisms. The persona is what the AI is asked to embody on every reply.
System prompt
The hidden instruction block the model receives before generating any reply. On Talaforge it includes the character's persona, the active scene rules, and the persistent memory snippet for this user.
Character card
A portable bundle of persona, opening message, default model and sampling parameters. Talaforge supports importing several community-standard card formats.
Token
The atomic unit a language model reads and writes. Roughly 4 characters of English text per token. Models have a maximum number of tokens they can consider at once.
Context window
The maximum amount of text a model can hold "in mind" for a single reply. Bigger windows let the model reference more of the recent conversation but cost more per call.
Persistent memory
Facts a character remembers about you across sessions. Distinct from the context window: memory is curated, editable, and injected into context on every reply. On Talaforge memory is per-character per-topic per-user and fully visible to you.
Branch (fork)
A divergence point in the conversation. Forking from a character reply creates an alternative thread that grows in parallel with the original; both are kept.
Regenerate
Asking for another version of the last reply without disturbing anything else. The character is given the same context and produces a new answer.
Model router
Infrastructure that picks an AI model for a given scene from a pool of available cards and fails over silently if one degrades. Includes weighted balancers and circuit-breakers.
Failover / circuit breaker
The router cuts off a degraded provider before it can poison a conversation with timeouts or refusals, and routes traffic to a healthy card instead.
Telegram Stars
Telegram's built-in micropayment unit. Purchased inside Telegram and spent on Talaforge upgrades like premium model time or character pack bundles.
Share link
An attribution-tracked URL that opens a chat with a creator's character. Stars spent by visitors earn the creator a referral share.
Creator economy
The system of share-link attribution, dashboard analytics and referral revenue that lets character authors on Talaforge earn from the engagement they generate.
Age gate
An in-bot verification step required before mature roleplay can be enabled. The public Talaforge landing is SFW; mature mode is opt-in after the gate.
Topic
A dedicated forum thread in Telegram that Talaforge uses to give each character its own room with its own pin strip, history and isolation.
RTL
Right-to-left writing direction, used for Arabic, Hebrew, Persian and Urdu. Talaforge mirrors UI elements natively for RTL languages.
Temperature
Sampling parameter that controls how predictable the model's replies are. Lower temperature is steadier; higher temperature is more surprising. Talaforge lets you set per-character defaults.
top_p / top_k / min_p
Sampling parameters that constrain which next-token candidates the model considers. Useful for tuning a character's register (cautious, expressive, terse, verbose).
Repetition penalty
Sampling parameter that discourages the model from repeating phrases. Useful for character voices that should sound fresh across many replies.
AI roleplay
Sustained fictional conversation in which the user takes a role and the AI takes another role, with consistent persona and narrative continuity.
AI companion
An AI character maintained as an ongoing relationship rather than a one-off conversation. Talaforge supports both companion-style and many-character use cases.
Lorebook
A library of world details that gets surfaced into context when relevant terms appear in conversation. Imported from several community-standard formats on Talaforge.
Sampling preset
A named bundle of sampling parameters tuned for a particular feel — slow-burn, banter, comedy, terse. Talaforge supports community-standard preset formats including SillyTavern's.
SFW / NSFW
Safe-for-work / not-safe-for-work content classification. The Talaforge public landing is SFW; mature content is gated behind in-bot age verification.
